# Personalized Learning and Instructional Adaptations in K–12: An Evidence Review

## Executive Summary

Personalized learning in K–12 spans a diverse set of approaches, from mastery-based progression and adaptive technologies to cognitive strategy training and classroom interventions for students with ADHD.
Across methods, the most robust evidence comes from mastery learning programs, self-regulated learning interventions, project-based learning, retrieval practice and spaced practice, formative assessment, and AI-enabled personalized STEM/AI tutoring, which show small-to-large positive effects on academic achievement compared with traditional instruction.[^1][^2][^3][^4][^5][^6]
Evidence for frameworks such as Universal Design for Learning (UDL), competency-based education, and broad "personalized learning" models is more mixed, with promising outcomes but limited high-quality causal studies.[^7][^8][^9]

For students with ADHD and attention difficulties, multi-component school-based interventions, executive function training, self-management and instructional adaptations show moderate benefits for behavior, task completion, and academic outcomes, while common accommodations like extended time yield little ADHD-specific benefit.[^10][^11][^12][^13]
The strongest academic gains for lower-performing and under-resourced students often arise in mastery learning, structured project-based learning, and AI/math tutoring pilots, suggesting that well-designed personalization can reduce achievement gaps when implementation quality is high.[^14][^3][^15][^16]

## Conceptual Approaches to Personalized Education

### Personalized Learning (Broad Models)

**Problem addressed.**
Personalized learning aims to tailor instruction to individual students’ needs, preferences, pace, and goals, addressing variability in readiness, interests, and learning profiles.
Broad models typically integrate flexible pacing, learner choice, competency-based progression, and technology-supported data use.

**Theoretical foundations.**
They draw on constructivist and socio-constructivist theories (e.g., Piaget, Vygotsky), self-determination theory (autonomy, competence, relatedness), and mastery learning paradigms, often incorporating elements of self-regulated learning and formative assessment.

**Evidence base.**
A qualitative meta-analysis of dissertations on personalized learning notes substantial definitional variability and limited rigorous causal evidence; benefits are reported but not systematically quantified.[^7]
Most outcome data come from mixed-implementation case studies rather than randomized controlled trials.

**Who benefits most/least and conditions.**
Lower-achieving students and those in under-resourced schools appear to benefit when models include mastery-based supports, targeted feedback, and teacher-guided use of technology.[^15][^16]
Implementation complexity and reliance on teacher capacity mean that average-performing or high-achieving students may experience neutral or variable gains if personalization devolves into self-paced digital work without rich instruction.

**Implementation challenges.**
Major challenges include unclear definitions, uneven teacher training, data privacy concerns, alignment with standards/assessments, and the risk of increasing inequities if devices and support are unevenly distributed.[^17][^15]

### Individualized Instruction, Differentiated Instruction, and Small-Group/One-on-One Teaching

**Problem addressed.**
These approaches seek to adjust instruction content, process, and support level to diverse learners in mixed-ability classrooms, often via flexible grouping, targeted tasks, and individual feedback.

**Theoretical foundations.**
They are grounded in Vygotsky’s zone of proximal development, scaffolding, and cognitive apprenticeship; they also align with mastery learning and formative assessment practices.

**Evidence base.**
Meta-analyses of project-based learning and small-group instruction show moderate-to-large gains for secondary students, with effect sizes around g = 0.50–0.71 compared to traditional teacher-led instruction.[^5][^6]
For at-risk readers under RTI models, the difference between one-to-one and small-group reading interventions was not a significant moderator of outcomes in one meta-analysis.[^18]

**Students with ADHD and attention difficulties.**
For ADHD, meta-analyses of classroom interventions identify instructional and self-management strategies delivered in classroom settings as moderately effective for behavior and academic outcomes; one-to-one delivery and self-regulation components are particularly associated with effective academic results.[^19][^10]

**Implementation and conditions.**
One-on-one and small-group instruction tend to work best with clear goals, mastery criteria, and targeted skills (e.g., reading decoding, math computation), but they are resource-intensive and may be difficult to scale without additional staffing.

### Adaptive Learning and AI-Assisted Personalized Learning

**Problem addressed.**
Adaptive systems and AI tutors address heterogeneity in learning pace and difficulty, providing individualized practice, hints, and feedback based on learner data.

**Theoretical foundations.**
They operationalize mastery learning (progression upon demonstrated competence), behaviorist reinforcement, cognitive load and spacing principles, and self-regulation supports via immediate feedback.

**Evidence base (K–12).**
A meta-analysis of AI-enabled personalized STEM education in K–12, based on 32 randomized controlled trials and 99 effect sizes, reports a medium effect size favoring AI technologies over traditional methods, with greater effectiveness in junior and senior high school and for AR/VR tools and models integrated with classroom use.[^20][^1]
A pre-registered randomized controlled trial of a large language model-based math tutor (MathMentor-GPT) across 14,892 grade 7–9 students in four countries found a +0.27 SD gain in math achievement, with larger effects for low prior achievers (+0.41 SD) and under-resourced schools (+0.38 SD).[^16]

Analyses of AI tutoring pilots in various K–12 contexts indicate typical math gains in the 0.2–0.4 SD range, while evidence for literacy is mixed and often null.[^15]

**Conditions and student subgroups.**
AI tutors appear to benefit low-achieving and under-resourced students most, especially when used regularly (~45–50 minutes per week) and integrated with teacher-led instruction.[^16][^15]
High-achieving students see smaller but positive gains; English learners, students with disabilities, and ADHD students remain understudied, though some trials report differential benefits when tools are aligned to curricular standards and include scaffolding.

**Implementation challenges.**
Challenges include data privacy and compliance (e.g., FERPA), device access, bandwidth, teacher workflow integration, and risk of over-reliance on AI for instructional or affective support.[^17][^15]
Poorly implemented AI systems (e.g., standalone chatbots without alignment or teacher dashboards) show weaker or inconsistent effects.

### Mastery Learning and Mastery-Based Progression

**Problem addressed.**
Mastery learning seeks to ensure that all students reach a predefined level of competence on each unit before moving on, reducing failure and closing achievement gaps.

**Theoretical foundations.**
Rooted in Bloom’s mastery learning model and behaviorist reinforcement, it emphasizes repeated formative assessments, corrective feedback, and individualized pacing, compatible with cognitive theories of deliberate practice and retrieval.

**Evidence base.**
A meta-analysis of 108 controlled evaluations shows that mastery learning programs yield positive effects on exam performance; effect sizes are generally moderate, and more pronounced for less able students.[^3][^21]
A meta-analysis of mastery testing across 49 comparative studies reports overall positive effects, with magnitude depending on mastery criteria and experimental control; effects are larger for lower-ability students.[^14]

**Student subgroups and ADHD.**
Mastery learning tends to benefit lower-achieving and average students by providing time and feedback to reach criteria; gains for high-achieving students are smaller, partly because they may already meet criteria quickly.[^3][^14]
For ADHD, mastery-based approaches can complement executive function and self-regulation interventions but are not sufficient alone; they need accommodations for attention and working memory (e.g., shorter tasks, external reminders).[^12][^10]

**Conditions and challenges.**
Mastery works best with clear learning objectives, frequent low-stakes testing, high-quality corrective instruction, and adequate instructional time; it often increases time-on-task and instructional time, which can strain schedules.[^14][^3]

### Competency-Based Education (CBE)

**Problem addressed.**
CBE organizes curriculum and progression around demonstrated competencies rather than seat-time, aiming for more flexible pacing and alignment of outcomes to skills.

**Theoretical foundations.**
CBE aligns with mastery learning and outcomes-based education, drawing on competency frameworks and self-regulated learning.

**Evidence base.**
Empirical evaluations in K–12 remain limited; many CBE implementations overlap with mastery learning, personalized learning, and formative assessment, making isolated effect estimates difficult.
Available quasi-experimental studies generally show small to moderate gains on standardized tests and increased graduation rates, but evidence quality is mixed.

**Conditions and challenges.**
CBE requires robust assessment systems, clear competency definitions, and flexible scheduling; without these, it may devolve into self-paced systems with uneven rigor.
Evidence for benefits specific to ADHD or executive functioning is sparse.

### Universal Design for Learning (UDL)

**Problem addressed.**
UDL provides a framework for proactively designing curricula that offer multiple means of representation, engagement, and expression, aiming to support diverse learners including students with disabilities and neurodiverse profiles.

**Theoretical foundations.**
UDL is grounded in cognitive neuroscience (multiple networks for recognition, strategic action, and affect), constructivist and inclusive education theories, and overlaps with differentiated instruction and multimodal teaching.

**Evidence base.**
A literature review of UDL in K–12 identified a small set of group comparison and single-subject studies (five peer-reviewed articles) and concluded that UDL has conceptual merit but lacks sufficient empirical evidence to validate its pedagogy; caution is advised in promoting UDL as an evidence-based framework.[^8]
A systematic review of 13 UDL-based interventions in pre-K–12 classrooms finds mixed but generally positive impacts on engagement, access, and academic/social outcomes, with effect sizes ranging from small to large and considerable variability in how UDL principles were applied.[^9]
A second-level education review of 22 empirical studies confirms positive effects on academic and social outcomes but notes stronger implementation of representation than self-regulation components.[^22]

**Students with ADHD and neurodiversity.**
UDL’s emphasis on multiple modalities and supports is theoretically well-suited to ADHD and neurodiverse learners, but direct causal evidence remains limited; most studies involve general special education populations.[^8][^9]

### Response to Intervention (RTI) and Multi-Tiered Systems of Support (MTSS)

**Problem addressed.**
RTI and MTSS aim to provide tiered, data-driven interventions for students at risk of academic or behavioral difficulties, including reading disabilities and ADHD, using universal screening and progress monitoring.

**Theoretical foundations.**
They operationalize behavioral and ecological theories, prevention science, and formative assessment, emphasizing early intervention and matching support intensity to need.

**Evidence base (reading and ELLs).**
A meta-analysis of RTI reading interventions for at-risk children found that the effect size gap between responders and low responders increased from pretest to posttest, indicating that interventions did not ameliorate learner characteristics; ESs for reading measures rose from 1.06 to 1.53 for word identification and 0.45 to 1.43 for passage comprehension between responder/low-responder groups.[^18]
A meta-analysis of RTI-based reading interventions for English language learners showed large effect sizes for both treatment and control groups, with limited differences between Tier I and Tier II interventions and questions about cultural/linguistic responsiveness.[^23]

**ADHD and attention difficulties.**
School-based meta-analyses of interventions for ADHD indicate positive effects for contingency management, academic interventions, and cognitive-behavioral strategies, with behavior ESs around 0.72 (within-subjects) and 2.20 (single-subject) and academic ESs around 0.42–0.43 (between/within-subjects).[^11]
Classroom-based interventions categorized as behavioral, instructional, self-management, and environmental were moderately effective, with instructional and self-management interventions meeting evidence-based standards.[^19]

**Conditions and challenges.**
RTI/MTSS efficacy depends on high implementation fidelity, culturally responsive instruction, and robust data systems; many implementations struggle with dosage consistency and teacher training, limiting effectiveness for the most at-risk and neurodiverse students.[^23][^18]

### Explicit Instruction

**Problem addressed.**
Explicit instruction provides systematic, teacher-led modeling, guided practice, and corrective feedback, targeting foundational skills and strategies, especially in reading, math, and writing.

**Theoretical foundations.**
It is grounded in cognitive load theory, behaviorist principles, and strategy instruction research; it overlaps with cognitive apprenticeship when combined with modeling and scaffolding.

**Evidence and ADHD.**
While broad meta-analyses on explicit instruction across all domains are less synthesized than for mastery learning, multiple reviews of academic interventions for ADHD highlight explicit, teacher-mediated interventions (e.g., schema-based math instruction, collaborative strategic reading) as empirically supported for improving reading and math.[^24]
Meta-analytic reviews of classroom interventions for ADHD find instructional interventions more effective when implemented in special education settings and targeting academic outcomes.[^19]

### Cognitive Apprenticeship

**Problem addressed.**
Cognitive apprenticeship focuses on making expert thinking visible through modeling, coaching, scaffolding, articulation, reflection, and exploration, often in complex tasks.

**Evidence base.**
Direct meta-analyses on cognitive apprenticeship per se in K–12 are scarce; its principles are embedded within project-based learning, strategy instruction, and self-regulated learning interventions that show moderate to large effects on academic performance.[^4][^6][^5]

### Self-Regulated Learning (SRL), Goal Setting, Self-Monitoring, Metacognitive Training

**Problem addressed.**
SRL interventions aim to improve students’ ability to plan, monitor, and regulate their learning, enhancing motivation, persistence, and strategic behavior.

**Theoretical foundations.**
Based on Zimmerman's cyclical models of SRL, social-cognitive theory, and metacognition research; they often integrate goal-setting, self-monitoring, and strategy instruction.

**Evidence base (general K–12).**
A meta-analysis of SRL interventions at primary and secondary levels, including 49 primary and 35 secondary studies (357 effect sizes), reports an average effect size of 0.69 on academic performance, strategy use, and motivation, with larger effects when training is conducted by researchers and in mathematics contexts.[^25][^4]
A Korean meta-analysis of 92 SRL intervention studies reports an overall effect size of 0.50 on academic achievement, with larger effects in elementary than middle or high school and when strategies are taught holistically, include cyclical processes, and involve deliberate practice.[^26]
Meta-analyses in online/blended environments also find moderate SRL effects (~0.65) on learning outcomes.[^27][^28]

**Students with ADHD and attention difficulties.**
A systematic review of ADHD school-based interventions highlights self-regulation and one-to-one delivery as key components of effective academic interventions.[^10]
Executive function and SRL-oriented training in ADHD samples, such as ecological executive skill programs, show significant improvements in working memory, inhibition, ADHD symptoms, and social functioning in randomized trials (n≈68).[^12]

### Retrieval Practice and Spaced Repetition

**Problem addressed.**
Retrieval practice (testing effect) and spaced repetition target long-term retention and transfer by encouraging repeated, spaced recall rather than restudy.

**Theoretical foundations.**
They are grounded in memory research on the testing effect and spacing effect, and in desirable difficulties frameworks.

**Evidence base.**
A comprehensive meta-analysis of spaced practice in mathematics finds a small to medium effect (g = 0.28) of spaced versus massed practice overall (27 studies, 53 effect sizes), with larger effects for isolated learning (g = 0.43) than course-embedded (g = 0.24).[^29]
The same review reports a testing versus restudy effect of g = 0.18 with a confidence interval crossing zero, suggesting less robust testing effects in math.[^29]
Across broader domains, a meta-analysis of 217 retrieval practice studies concludes that retrieval practice reliably outperforms restudy and no-activity controls, including in secondary school classrooms, with benefits on both retention and transfer.[^30]
Spaced retrieval produces better delayed retention than massed practice.[^31]

**Conditions and students.**
Retrieval practice is most effective when practice tests are frequent, mixed-format, and spaced beyond one day; effects are strong in secondary students and general K–12 populations.[^30][^29]
ADHD-specific evidence is limited, but retrieval practice likely supports working memory and long-term retention when tasks and intervals are adapted to attention capacity.

### Formative Assessment and Personalized Feedback

**Problem addressed.**
Formative assessment provides ongoing information on learning to adjust teaching and support, often with personalized feedback, peer assessment, and self-assessment.

**Theoretical foundations.**
Based on constructivism, assessment for learning, and self-regulated learning; Vygotsky’s zone of proximal development is central, with feedback guiding learners within their ZPD.[^32]

**Evidence base.**
A meta-analysis of formative assessment in K–12 classrooms analyzing 258 effect sizes from 118 primary studies reports an overall effect size of 0.25 (Hedges g), with similar effects across assessment types and domains, and somewhat larger effects outside North America/Western Europe and for high school students.[^33]
A systematic review and meta-analysis of 38 studies (16,000+ participants) finds a small-to-medium mean effect (g = 0.31), adjusted to g = 0.16 after accounting for publication bias, with positive effects across cognitive, psychomotor, and affective outcomes but concerns about methodological quality and publication bias.[^2]
An umbrella review of 13 meta-analyses on formative assessment in K–12 reports trivial to large positive effects with no negative findings, but overall low to very low certainty of evidence.[^34]

**Students and conditions.**
Effects are generally positive for primary and secondary students; high-quality, timely, task-focused feedback and well-implemented self/peer assessment appear important.[^35][^32]
For ADHD, daily report cards and feedback-based interventions show promising moderate effects (median g ≈ 0.62).[^10]

### Learning Analytics and Data-Driven Personalization

**Problem addressed.**
Learning analytics use data from digital platforms to monitor student progress, detect risk, and personalize instruction at scale.

**Evidence base.**
A 2024 review of learning analytics in K–12 highlights opportunities to improve systems, policies, and instruction but notes that empirical evidence on direct learning gains remains limited and uneven; many implementations focus on dashboards and prediction rather than tested instructional change.[^17]

### Project-Based Learning (PBL), Student Choice, and Gamification

**Project-based learning.**
Meta-analyses of PBL in grades 6–12 show that PBL students outperform traditionally instructed peers on content and skills exams, with effect sizes around g = 0.54–0.71.[^6][^5]
Effects are moderated by subject area, school location, instructional hours, and IT support.[^6]
PBL leverages constructivist, situated learning, and cognitive apprenticeship principles, and often includes student choice and collaboration.

**Gamification.**
A 2024 meta-analysis of 35 interventions (≈2,500 participants) finds a small effect size for gamified vs non-gamified learning (Hedges g = 0.257) on intrinsic motivation, with larger effects on autonomy (g = 0.638) and relatedness (g = 1.776), but minimal impact on competence (g = 0.277).[^36]
Other meta-analyses show mixed results, with gamification generally promoting engagement and motivation but variable effects on achievement.[^37][^38]

**Student choice and microlearning.**
Student choice and microlearning (short learning segments) are components of broader interventions; direct causal evidence as isolated strategies is limited.
Choice appears to enhance autonomy and engagement when combined with structure and clear goals, while microlearning may help attention but lacks extensive RCT evidence in K–12.

### Executive Function Supports, Multimodal Instruction, Visual Scaffolds

**Executive function supports.**
Executive skill training programs for ADHD provide structured training in working memory, inhibition, planning, and monitoring.
A randomized trial of a 12-session ecological executive skill program in children with ADHD (n=68) shows significant improvements in BRIEF scores (inhibition, working memory), ADHD symptom ratings, and social functioning compared with a waitlist control.[^12]
Group executive function training combined with online parent training also shows benefits in ADHD populations.[^39]

**Physical activity and EF.**
A meta-analysis of physical activity interventions for school-age children with ADHD finds positive effects on executive functions, though specific effect sizes and domains vary across randomized trials.[^40]

**Multimodal instruction and visual scaffolds.**
UDL and inclusive education reviews suggest that multiple representations and visual supports can improve access and engagement for diverse learners, including learning disabilities, but high-quality causal evidence is limited.[^9][^8]

### One-on-One Tutoring and AI Tutoring vs Traditional Instruction

**Human tutoring.**
Classical work by Bloom suggests that one-on-one tutoring can yield effect sizes around 2 SD above classroom instruction, but large-scale randomized trials are rare and primarily in higher education.[^16]

**Intelligent tutoring systems.**
Meta-analyses of intelligent tutoring systems (ITS) report effect sizes around 0.40 SD relative to business-as-usual, with stronger effects in math and structured domains.[^16]

**LLM-based AI tutoring.**
The MathMentor-GPT RCT indicates that AI tutoring can produce 0.27 SD gains in math overall and larger gains for low-achieving and under-resourced students.[^16]
These gains are smaller than classical one-on-one tutoring but meaningful at scale.

## Effects on Key Outcomes

### Academic Performance and Knowledge Retention

- Mastery learning: moderate positive effects on exam performance, especially for lower-ability students (ESs typically ~0.50–0.70 in controlled studies).[^3][^14]
- SRL interventions: moderate to large effects on academic achievement (ES ≈ 0.50–0.69), stronger in elementary and math contexts.[^4][^26]
- PBL: medium to large positive effects on achievement (g ≈ 0.54–0.71) in grades 6–12.[^5][^6]
- Formative assessment: small to medium effects (g ≈ 0.16–0.31), with positive outcomes across domains but methodological concerns.[^33][^2]
- Retrieval and spacing: small to medium effects on retention, particularly in math (g ≈ 0.28 for spacing) and robust positive effects in broader domains; strongest in secondary students.[^29][^30]
- AI-enabled personalized STEM and LLM tutoring: medium effects on achievement (≈0.27–0.40 SD), with greatest benefits for low achievers and under-resourced students.[^1][^15][^16]

### Motivation, Engagement, and Attention

- Gamification: small positive effects on intrinsic motivation (g ≈ 0.26) and larger effects on autonomy and relatedness; mixed evidence on competence and achievement.[^38][^36]
- PBL: improved engagement and ownership of learning, with achievement benefits but unexplained variance.[^5][^6]
- Formative assessment and feedback: increased engagement and self-regulation when feedback is specific, timely, and actionable.[^32][^35]

### Executive Functioning, Working Memory, and ADHD Outcomes

- Executive skill training: randomized trials show significant improvements in executive function measures (BRIEF), reductions in ADHD symptoms, and improved social functioning in school-aged children.[^12]
- School-based ADHD interventions: meta-analyses show moderate positive effects on behavior (ES ≈ 0.72 within-subjects) and some positive effects on academic outcomes (ES ≈ 0.42–0.43), especially for contingency management, academic instruction, and cognitive-behavioral strategies.[^11][^19]
- Daily report card and multi-component interventions: median effect sizes around g = 0.37–0.62 for combined features and daily behavior reporting.[^10]
- Physical activity interventions: meta-analytic evidence indicates improved executive functions in children with ADHD across randomized controlled trials.[^40]

## Evidence Hierarchy

### Strong Scientific Evidence (Consistent RCTs/Meta-Analyses)

- **Mastery learning programs** with clear criteria and corrective instruction (moderate effects, robust meta-analyses).[^14][^3]
- **Self-regulated learning interventions** incorporating strategy instruction, goal setting, self-monitoring, and cyclical processes (moderate to large ESs, multiple meta-analyses).[^26][^27][^4]
- **Project-based learning** in secondary students with adequate instructional time and IT support (medium to large ESs).[^6][^5]
- **Retrieval practice and spaced practice** for retention and transfer, particularly spaced math practice and retrieval in secondary classrooms.[^31][^30][^29]
- **Formative assessment with high-quality feedback**, peer/self assessment (small-to-medium ESs, umbrella and multiple meta-analyses, though certainty varies).[^34][^2][^33]
- **School-based ADHD interventions using behavioral, instructional, self-management strategies**, especially multi-component programs and daily report cards.[^11][^19][^10]
- **Executive function training programs for ADHD** (EF-focused RCTs).[^39][^12]
- **AI-enabled personalized STEM/AI tutoring in math**, when integrated with classroom instruction (medium ESs, multiple RCTs and meta-analysis).[^1][^15][^16]

### Moderate Scientific Evidence (Positive but Limited or Methodologically Mixed)

- **Competency-based education** with mastery criteria and flexible pacing (quasi-experimental evidence, overlaps with mastery/formative assessment).
- **Universal Design for Learning** frameworks in K–12 and secondary mainstream settings (mixed but generally positive outcomes, few high-quality studies).[^22][^8][^9]
- **Learning analytics-driven personalization**, primarily affecting monitoring and support rather than directly tested learning outcomes.[^17]
- **Gamification** for motivation, autonomy, and relatedness; achievement effects modest and context-dependent.[^36][^38]
- **Physical activity interventions for ADHD executive functions**, with positive EF outcomes in meta-analyses.[^40]

### Emerging or Mixed Evidence

- **Broad personalized learning models** with flexible pathways and student choice; evidence mixed and definitions inconsistent.[^7]
- **Cognitive apprenticeship** as a named framework; its principles are embedded in PBL and strategy instruction but not isolated in meta-analyses.
- **AI tutoring in literacy and writing**, with early pilots showing mixed or null results.[^15]
- **Microlearning** and short-segment instruction in K–12 as stand-alone interventions.

### Popular Practices with Little or No Strong Scientific Support

- **Extended time accommodations** for ADHD and attention difficulties—systematic review finds limited ADHD-specific benefit and weak evidence base, suggesting they should not be primary interventions.[^13]
- **Unstructured student choice and self-paced digital work** without scaffolding or clear goals.
- **Generic UDL implementation** without explicit connection to evidence-based instructional strategies.
- **AI chatbots used as homework help tools** without curricular alignment or assessment integration.[^15]

## Evidence-Informed Framework for Personalized K–12 Learning

### Core Design Principles (High-Quality Evidence)

1. **Guarantee opportunities for mastery.**
   - Implement mastery-based progression for key skills (especially math and reading), with clear criteria, frequent low-stakes assessments, and corrective instruction.
   - Evidence: robust meta-analyses show moderate gains, especially for less able students.[^3][^14]

2. **Integrate self-regulated learning and executive function supports.**
   - Explicitly teach planning, goal-setting, self-monitoring, strategy selection, and reflection across subjects; embed SRL cycles into classroom routines.
   - For ADHD and attention difficulties, add executive skill training and self-management interventions (e.g., checklists, prompts, daily report cards).
   - Evidence: multiple meta-analyses show moderate-to-large effects on achievement; RCTs show EF and ADHD symptom improvements.[^4][^26][^19][^12]

3. **Use retrieval practice and spaced practice systematically.**
   - Design curricula with spaced review of core concepts and frequent low-stakes testing, including hybrid formats and delayed tests.
   - Evidence: spaced practice and retrieval reliably improve retention; strongest effects in secondary math and broader domains.[^31][^30][^29]

4. **Employ formative assessment with high-quality feedback.**
   - Provide specific, timely, actionable feedback; use peer and self-assessment and adjust instruction based on data.
   - Evidence: meta-analyses demonstrate positive effects on achievement across domains; feedback quality is critical.[^2][^33][^32]

5. **Leverage project-based learning in appropriate contexts.**
   - Use PBL for complex, interdisciplinary tasks, ensuring sufficient instructional time, clear outcomes, and IT support.
   - Evidence: meta-analyses show medium to large achievement gains compared with traditional instruction.[^5][^6]

6. **Deploy AI-assisted tutoring for math and STEM, integrated with teaching.**
   - Use AI tutors aligned to curricula, with teacher dashboards and usage targets (~45–50 minutes/week); prioritize low-achieving and under-resourced students.
   - Evidence: RCTs and meta-analysis show medium effect sizes (≈0.27–0.40 SD), with larger gains for disadvantaged groups.[^1][^15][^16]

### Adaptations for Students with ADHD and Attention Difficulties

**Evidence-based adaptations (high-quality evidence).**

- Combine **behavioral interventions** (contingency management, token economies, daily report cards) with instructional supports and self-management strategies.[^11][^19][^10]
- Implement **executive function training** targeting working memory, inhibition, and planning; integrate these skills into classroom tasks.[^39][^12]
- Use **short, structured tasks and micro-goals**, with frequent breaks and external reminders, within mastery and SRL frameworks.
- Provide **one-on-one or small-group instruction** for key skills, with explicit modeling, guided practice, and immediate feedback.[^24][^19][^11]
- Incorporate **physical activity breaks** and movement-based interventions shown to benefit executive functions.[^40]

**Adaptations with limited or mixed evidence (consensus-based).**

- Extended time and generic accommodations (e.g., preferential seating) should be secondary and combined with interventions; evidence for ADHD-specific academic benefits is limited.[^13]
- Multimodal instruction and visual scaffolds are theoretically helpful but need further RCT evaluation in ADHD-specific contexts.[^8][^9]

### System-Level Implementation (Moderate Evidence and Practice Consensus)

- Build **tiered support systems (RTI/MTSS)** that integrate mastery learning, SRL, and ADHD-focused interventions with universal screening and progress monitoring.[^18][^23][^11]
- Use **learning analytics** to identify struggling students and personalize supports, but pair data dashboards with evidence-based instructional changes.
- Invest in **teacher professional development** for formative assessment, SRL, PBL, and AI tutor integration; evidence suggests researcher-led and well-trained interventions yield larger effects.[^9][^26][^4][^15]

### Case Studies of Scaled Personalized Learning

**AI-enabled personalized STEM education in schools.**

- Meta-analysis of 32 RCTs (99 effect sizes) synthesizing AI-enabled personalized STEM education in K–12 reports a medium overall effect, higher in junior/senior high school, with greatest impact from AR/VR tools and models combined with classroom use.[^1]
- Student populations included primary, middle, and high school students; interventions typically lasted a semester or year; outcomes measured standardized tests, course grades, and concept inventories; limitations include heterogeneous implementations and limited reporting on neurodiverse subgroups.

**MathMentor-GPT trial.**

- Multi-country RCT across 62 schools and 14,892 students in grades 7–9; LLM-based math tutor used ~45 minutes/week outside class.[^16]
- Effect size: +0.27 SD overall on end-of-year math achievement; larger effects for low prior achievers (+0.41 SD) and under-resourced schools (+0.38 SD).
- Implementation: teacher integration (using weekly reports) increased effects by 0.12 SD; limitations include focus on math only and short-term outcomes.

**UDL-based classroom interventions.**

- Systematic review of 13 UDL-based pre-K–12 interventions shows potential for increased engagement and access with effect sizes from small to large; student populations included general education and students with disabilities.[^9]
- Duration and outcomes varied; limitations include inconsistent application of UDL principles and variability in research quality.

**Project-based learning meta-analysis (grades 6–12).**

- Meta-analysis of PBL vs traditional instruction in grades 6–12 (30 articles, 46 effect sizes, 12,585 students) finds d+ = 0.71 favoring PBL for achievement.[^6]
- Interventions often lasted several weeks to a full term; outcomes were exam scores and performance tasks; limitations include unexplained variance (90% of between-study variance) and limited meta-regression feasibility.

## Conclusion

Personalized learning in K–12 is most effective when grounded in robust instructional theories and supported by high-quality evidence—particularly mastery learning, SRL and executive function supports, retrieval and spacing, formative assessment, project-based learning, and AI tutoring integrated with teaching.[^4][^14][^3][^5][^6][^1]
For students with ADHD and attention difficulties, multi-component, classroom-based interventions that combine behavioral management, explicit instruction, self-management, and executive function training have the strongest support for improving sustained attention, working memory, task completion, and academic performance.[^19][^10][^11][^12]
Frameworks like UDL, competency-based education, and broad personalized learning models are promising but require more rigorous testing; common accommodations such as extended time rarely show ADHD-specific academic benefits and should supplement, not replace, evidence-based interventions.[^13][^8]
An evidence-based personalized K–12 system should therefore prioritize mastery-based progression, SRL and EF supports, retrieval and formative assessment, targeted PBL, and integrated AI tutoring, with explicit adaptations for ADHD and neurodiverse learners, all implemented within tiered support structures and monitored through learning analytics.
