# Personalized Learning in K–12: Evidence for Children and Adolescents, with a Focus on ADHD and Attention Difficulties

## Executive Summary

Personalized and adaptive approaches to K–12 education show consistently positive, but heterogeneous, effects on academic achievement, motivation, and engagement, with effect sizes typically in the small-to-moderate range and larger benefits for lower-achieving students and those with learning or attentional difficulties. Strongest evidence exists for mastery learning, retrieval practice, self-regulated learning interventions, Response to Intervention (RTI) / Multi-Tiered Systems of Support (MTSS), and project-based/design-based learning; emerging high-quality randomized evidence supports AI-assisted tutoring and learning analytics–driven personalization.[^1][^2][^3][^4][^5][^6][^7][^8]

For students with ADHD and attention difficulties, behavioral classroom interventions, executive-function and working-memory training, structured explicit instruction, and school-based psychosocial programs show small-to-moderate improvements in on-task behavior, task completion, and some academic outcomes, while medication yields modest direct gains in achievement and larger effects on behavior. Evidence for UDL, competency-based education, and broad “personalized learning” initiatives is promising but mixed, with variable fidelity and limited large-scale randomized studies.[^9][^10][^11][^12][^13][^14][^15]

An evidence-based personalized learning system for K–12 should combine: (a) mastery-oriented pacing and formative assessment; (b) frequent retrieval practice and spaced review; (c) explicit, structured instruction with scaffolded practice; (d) RTI/MTSS tiered supports; (e) self-regulation, goal-setting, and metacognitive strategy coaching; (f) project-based and design-based tasks for deeper learning; and (g) AI or learning analytics tools that augment, not replace, teacher judgment, with specific adaptations for ADHD such as shortened tasks, visual scaffolds, behavioral supports, and executive-function training.[^3][^11][^13][^4][^6][^7][^8][^1]


## Conceptual Foundations of Personalized Education

Personalized, individualized, and differentiated instruction all aim to tailor teaching to individual learners’ needs, but they differ in scope and mechanism. Personalized learning typically refers to systems where students have individualized goals, pacing, and often pathways, sometimes supported by technology; individualized instruction focuses on adapting tasks and supports for each student; differentiated instruction emphasizes systematically varying content, process, product, and environment within the same classroom to address learner variability. Adaptive learning and AI tutoring are technology-mediated forms of personalization that adjust difficulty and feedback in real time based on performance data.[^16][^17][^18][^2][^3]

Underlying theories include constructivism, socio-cultural views of learning (e.g., Vygotsky’s zone of proximal development), information-processing models of memory, self-regulated learning theory, mastery learning theory (Bloom), and multi-tiered support frameworks (RTI/MTSS). For ADHD and executive-function challenges, theories emphasize deficits in behavioral inhibition, sustained attention, working memory, and self-regulation, motivating structured scaffolds and environmental supports.[^17][^19][^20][^21][^7][^1]


## Evidence on Major Approaches to Personalized Education

### Differentiated Instruction (DI)

**Problem and theory.** DI is designed to address learner diversity in mixed-ability classrooms by tailoring content, process, and products to students’ readiness, interests, and learning profiles, grounded in constructivist and learner-centered theories. It seeks to reduce achievement gaps while maintaining high expectations for all students.[^16][^17]

**Evidence strength.** Reviews and meta-analyses suggest DI can modestly improve achievement, motivation, and engagement, but effect sizes vary widely and implementation quality is uneven. A systematic review focusing on high-achieving students in mixed-ability classrooms (49 studies, 2000–2019) found predominantly positive effects on academic achievement and motivational-affective outcomes, but with substantial heterogeneity and limited proactive use of DI for gifted learners. Qualitative syntheses report enhanced engagement and motivation when teachers use flexible grouping, scaffolding, and tiered tasks.[^22][^23][^24][^25][^17][^16]

**Beneficiaries and conditions.** DI appears most beneficial for students at both ends of the achievement distribution—low-performing and high-achieving students—when implemented with strong formative assessment and flexible grouping. It works best in classrooms with smaller student–teacher ratios, sustained professional development, and supportive leadership; it struggles under large class sizes, rigid curricula, limited assessment literacy, or superficial implementation. Evidence specific to ADHD is limited, but DI strategies such as chunking tasks, providing choice, and adjusting difficulty can align with recommended accommodations for attention difficulties.[^23][^25][^11][^20][^16]

**Implementation challenges and outcomes.** Teachers often report DI as conceptually valuable but difficult to implement consistently due to planning time, assessment demands, and classroom management complexity. When implemented well, studies report small-to-moderate gains in literacy and mathematics, improved engagement, and reduced achievement gaps, though quantitative effect sizes are less consistently reported than in mastery learning or RTI meta-analyses.[^25][^22][^23][^16]


### Mastery Learning and Mastery-Based Progression

**Problem and theory.** Mastery learning aims to ensure that nearly all students reach high levels of achievement by adapting time and instructional support until mastery is achieved, rather than allowing wide variation in attainment. It rests on Bloom’s mastery learning theory, which posits that with appropriate conditions—high-quality instruction, corrective feedback, and sufficient time—most students can reach similar performance levels.[^26][^21]

**Evidence strength.** Multiple meta-analyses of group-based mastery learning (e.g., Guskey & Gates 1986; Guskey & Pigott 1988; Kulik & Kulik 1986) report consistently positive, moderate-to-large effects on achievement. A synthesis of 771 studies involving 11,383 students and 659 effects reports a weighted mean effect size of 0.67, with higher effects in elementary grades (d ≈ 0.94) and for lower-ability students (d ≈ 0.96). These findings indicate mastery learning is among the more powerful instructional influences in Hattie’s large meta-analysis of achievement.[^27][^28][^4][^21][^5]

**Beneficiaries and conditions.** Effects are strongest for elementary students and lower-achieving learners, suggesting mastery-based progression particularly benefits those who struggle under traditional pacing. High school students also benefit (d ≈ 0.72), though the magnitude is slightly lower, possibly due to more complex content and constraints on time. Successful implementation requires frequent formative assessments, clear mastery criteria, corrective instruction, and flexible time allocation; without these, mastery learning reduces to frequent testing without adequate support and loses impact.[^4][^21][^5][^29]

**Implementation challenges and outcomes.** Challenges include curriculum alignment, grading policies, and scheduling in secondary schools. Nevertheless, when implemented with fidelity, mastery learning improves test performance, long-term retention, student attitudes toward learning, and participation, and tends to narrow achievement gaps.[^21][^5][^29][^26][^4]


### Competency-Based Education (CBE) / Competency-Based Progression

**Problem and theory.** CBE aims to replace time-based progression with advancement based on demonstrated competencies, emphasizing clear learning targets, flexible pacing, and authentic assessment. It is rooted in outcomes-based education, mastery learning, and standards-based reform, and often incorporates personalized pathways.[^14][^30]

**Evidence strength.** A systematic literature review of K–12 CBE implementation and outcomes (25 studies, 2000–2019) found mixed evidence regarding claims that CBE improves academic achievement, intrinsic motivation, engagement, and other outcomes. Facilitators and barriers were relatively consistent across contexts, but many studies used quasi-experimental designs, lacked robust measures of competency, and struggled to isolate CBE effects from concurrent reforms.[^15][^14]

**Beneficiaries and conditions.** Some district and state pilots report improved progress rates, reduced course failures, and greater student ownership of learning, particularly for students who previously failed courses or were off track to graduate. However, benefits depend heavily on assessment quality, clarity of competencies, teacher understanding, and system-level supports; without these, CBE can devolve into checklist-based grading without substantive instructional change.[^30][^14][^15]

**Implementation challenges and outcomes.** Key challenges include developing high-quality performance assessments, ensuring equity in pacing (avoiding students being left behind), integrating CBE with accountability systems, and managing complex data systems. Overall, CBE’s evidence base is emerging and mixed, with promising results but limited high-quality randomized trials; its strongest support is conceptual and policy-oriented rather than empirical.[^14][^30]


### Universal Design for Learning (UDL)

**Problem and theory.** UDL is a framework for designing curricula that provide multiple means of engagement, representation, and action/expression to support diverse learners, including students with disabilities and attention difficulties. It is grounded in cognitive neuroscience of variability, CAST’s UDL guidelines, and principles of accessibility and inclusion.[^10][^31]

**Evidence strength.** A systematic review of UDL-based instruction in pre-K–12 (13 studies) found that UDL-aligned interventions can increase engagement, access to general education curriculum, and academic and social outcomes for students with disabilities, but effect sizes vary widely and methodological quality is inconsistent. Another review suggests UDL can enhance outcomes in higher education, but K–12 evidence remains limited and often involves small samples or nonrandomized designs.[^32][^10]

**Beneficiaries and conditions.** UDL appears promising for students with disabilities, including ADHD and neurodiverse learners, by embedding supports like visual scaffolds, multimodal instruction, and flexible response options. Benefits are most likely when UDL principles are integrated into whole-school design, supported by professional learning and collaborative planning; piecemeal application or superficial labeling as “UDL” without explicit alignment tends to yield weaker results.[^31][^33][^10]

**Implementation challenges and outcomes.** Challenges include translating broad UDL guidelines into concrete classroom practices, ensuring teachers have adequate training, and evaluating fidelity. Quantitative evidence points to small-to-moderate gains in engagement and achievement, but heterogeneity and limited study numbers place UDL in the moderate-to-emerging evidence tier.[^33][^31]


### Response to Intervention (RTI) and Multi-Tiered Systems of Support (MTSS)

**Problem and theory.** RTI and MTSS are multi-tiered frameworks aimed at preventing and remediating academic and behavioral difficulties through universal high-quality instruction (Tier 1), targeted small-group interventions (Tier 2), and intensive individualized supports (Tier 3). They rely on continuous progress monitoring, data-driven decision-making, and early intervention.[^34][^35]

**Evidence strength.** Meta-analytic syntheses of RTI and related intervention programs report moderate-to-large effects on student outcomes. A Visible Learning summary based on eight meta-analyses (180 studies; 11,231 students; 689 effects) reports a weighted mean effect size of 0.73, indicating RTI is a powerful influence on achievement when properly implemented. An RTI meta-analysis for children at risk of reading disabilities (13 studies; 107 posttest effect sizes) found posttest effect sizes around 0.76 between responders and low responders, with gains particularly in word identification and comprehension, though pre-existing differences were not fully mitigated.[^36][^7][^37]

**Beneficiaries and conditions.** RTI and MTSS particularly benefit struggling readers and students at risk of learning disabilities, including some children with ADHD whose academic difficulties stem partly from skill deficits. Tier 2 interventions often show medium-to-large effects (e.g., effect sizes ≈ 1.10 in Korean implementations), while Tier 1 universal instruction yields smaller but meaningful gains. Success depends on high-quality core instruction, evidence-based Tier 2/3 programs, reliable progress monitoring, and coordinated team structures; poorly implemented RTI, especially with weak Tier 1, can fail to reduce nonresponse rates and may delay needed special education services.[^7][^35][^38][^37][^36]

**Implementation challenges and outcomes.** Challenges include resource allocation, staff training, data infrastructure, and balancing identification and intervention roles. Large-scale district MTSS reforms (e.g., Wake County RCT) are still under evaluation, but early reports emphasize potential improvements in test scores and behavior when MTSS is phased in systematically. Overall, RTI/MTSS has strong empirical support for improving academic performance and reducing behavioral problems.[^39][^35][^34]


### Explicit Instruction

**Problem and theory.** Explicit instruction involves systematic, teacher-led modeling, guided practice, and independent practice with clear objectives, frequent feedback, and high opportunities to respond, grounded in direct instruction and cognitive load theory. It is particularly recommended for students with ADHD and learning disabilities who benefit from structured, predictable routines and explicit teaching of strategies.[^35][^27]

**Evidence strength.** While explicit instruction is embedded in many RTI and mastery learning programs rather than studied as a standalone label, meta-analytic work on direct instruction and structured teaching shows moderate-to-large effects, especially in reading and mathematics for elementary and middle school students. For ADHD, behaviorally oriented classroom interventions that include explicit instruction, clear rules, and reinforcement show small-to-moderate improvements in on-task behavior and work completion.[^11][^20][^27][^7]

**Beneficiaries and conditions.** Explicit instruction benefits struggling learners, students with ADHD, and those with executive-function challenges by reducing cognitive load, making expectations transparent, and allowing repeated practice. It is particularly effective when combined with strategy instruction (e.g., reading comprehension strategies, organizational skills) and when sessions are brief and interleaved with active responding, which aligns with attention capacities.[^13][^20][^9][^11]

**Implementation challenges and outcomes.** Challenges include perceptions that explicit instruction may reduce student autonomy or creativity if misapplied, and the need for high-quality lesson design to avoid rote teaching. Nevertheless, when balanced with opportunities for discussion and application, explicit instruction consistently improves academic outcomes and is considered a high-impact, evidence-based practice.[^27]


### Cognitive Apprenticeship

**Problem and theory.** Cognitive apprenticeship models learning as guided participation in expert practices, emphasizing modeling, coaching, scaffolding, articulation, reflection, and gradual fading of support. It stems from socio-constructivist theories and is closely related to project-based and design-based learning.[^6][^17]

**Evidence strength.** Specific meta-analyses labeled “cognitive apprenticeship” are scarce; however, interventions that incorporate apprenticeship elements—such as project-based and design-based learning—show medium-to-large positive effects on achievement across K–12. These approaches personalize learning by situating tasks in real-world contexts and supporting students’ strategic thinking.[^8][^6]

**Beneficiaries and conditions.** Cognitive apprenticeship is particularly beneficial for upper elementary, middle, and high school students when applied to complex problem-solving domains (e.g., STEM projects) and when combined with explicit strategy coaching and formative feedback. For ADHD, apprenticeship-style scaffolding can support engagement and motivation, though high demands on planning and sustained attention require careful task structuring and support.[^20][^13][^6][^8]

**Implementation challenges and outcomes.** Implementation requires substantial teacher expertise in scaffolding, assessment of process skills, and project design; without these, projects may become unfocused or inequitable. Overall, evidence supports cognitive-apprenticeship-informed practices as effective personalization mechanisms for deeper learning.[^6][^8]


### Self-Regulated Learning (SRL) and Metacognitive Training

**Problem and theory.** SRL interventions target students’ ability to plan, monitor, and regulate their learning through goals, strategy use, self-monitoring, and reflection, rooted in Zimmerman and Pintrich’s SRL models and metacognition theory. These are particularly relevant for students with executive-function challenges.[^40][^1]

**Evidence strength.** Meta-analyses of SRL interventions in primary and secondary school report average effect sizes around 0.69 on academic performance, strategy use, and motivation. A meta-analysis of SRL interventions in online and blended contexts found moderate effects (Q ≈ 0.65) on learning outcomes, with no significant differences by school level or context. A meta-analysis of SRL components across childhood and adolescence found small but systematic correlations with achievement (r ≈ 0.20 for metacognitive processes; r ≈ 0.11 for cognitive strategies), moderated by subject and measurement type. Recent meta-analyses highlight the role of feedback in supporting SRL.[^41][^42][^43][^40][^1]

**Beneficiaries and conditions.** SRL training benefits both average and high-performing students, with particular advantages for those who initially lack effective study strategies. For ADHD and attention difficulties, SRL and executive-function interventions can improve working memory, planning, and self-monitoring, though effects on core symptoms are modest. SRL works best when embedded in subject-specific instruction (e.g., mathematics or reading) and when teachers model strategies and provide ongoing feedback.[^42][^44][^9][^1][^13][^20]

**Implementation challenges and outcomes.** Challenges include teachers’ limited familiarity with SRL theory and the need for time to teach strategies explicitly. Nonetheless, SRL interventions show consistent moderate improvements in academic achievement, motivation, and self-efficacy, positioning them as strong candidates for personalized learning systems.[^1]


### Retrieval Practice and Spaced Repetition

**Problem and theory.** Retrieval practice involves actively recalling information from memory (typically through low-stakes testing), while spaced repetition distributes practice over time; both are grounded in cognitive psychology of memory and the testing effect. They target long-term retention and transfer.[^45][^46]

**Evidence strength.** A comprehensive meta-analysis of 217 retrieval-practice studies found that retrieval practice consistently outperforms restudy and no-activity controls, with benefits for both retention and transfer. Effects are strongest when practice and final test formats align and when practice includes hybrid question types; benefits are larger with delays longer than one day. Classroom-based studies confirm that retrieval practice improves learning in “real” school settings, with particularly strong effects for secondary students. Narrative reviews focusing on school-aged children similarly report robust positive impacts across subjects.[^47][^46][^48][^49][^45]

Spaced learning models (e.g., SMART Spaces 24/10) show that specific spacing schedules during examination revision significantly improve test performance compared to massed study in randomized pilot trials. Interleaved practice—a related spacing strategy—has demonstrated advantages for mathematics problem-solving in randomized controlled studies.[^50][^51]

**Beneficiaries and conditions.** Retrieval practice is effective across age groups, with high effect sizes for secondary students and meaningful benefits for elementary students when tests are appropriately designed. It likely benefits ADHD students’ retention by leveraging frequent, brief, low-stakes quizzes that can sustain attention and provide immediate feedback, although direct ADHD-specific meta-analyses are limited. Spaced repetition is particularly important for long-term retention and may support students with working-memory limitations by reducing forgetting between sessions.[^51][^49][^45][^50][^20]

**Implementation challenges and outcomes.** Challenges include shifting teacher and student perceptions of testing from summative to formative, ensuring quizzes are low stakes, and integrating retrieval practice into ongoing instruction. When implemented, retrieval practice yields medium effects on retention and some transfer outcomes, improves metacognitive accuracy, and can enhance engagement when combined with gamification or immediate feedback.[^46][^48][^52][^45]


### Project-Based Learning (PBL) and Design-Based Learning (DBL)

**Problem and theory.** PBL and DBL are learner-centered approaches that organize instruction around complex projects or design challenges, promoting application of knowledge, collaboration, and problem-solving, grounded in constructivist and experiential learning theories.[^53][^8][^6]

**Evidence strength.** A meta-analysis of PBL effects on academic achievement in grades 6–12 found that PBL students outperformed traditionally instructed peers with an effect size g ≈ 0.54 across subjects and grade levels, with adjusted effects around g ≈ 0.50 after accounting for publication bias. Another meta-analysis of PBL (30 articles; 46 effect sizes; 12,585 students) reported a mean weighted effect size d ≈ 0.71, indicating medium-to-large positive effects compared with traditional instruction.[^54][^53][^6]

DBL meta-analyses involving 37 articles and 52 effect sizes found a large positive effect (ES ≈ 0.60) on achievement, with stronger effects in science (≈ 0.70) than mathematics (≈ 0.42). Effects tend to be larger when control groups are from the same school and when assignment is nonrandom, suggesting some contextual influence.[^8]

**Beneficiaries and conditions.** PBL and DBL particularly benefit students’ deeper conceptual understanding, problem-solving, and attitudes towards learning, especially in STEM disciplines and when adequate instructional time and scaffolding are provided. These approaches can be highly engaging for ADHD and neurodiverse learners due to hands-on, authentic tasks, but they may exacerbate executive-function challenges if projects are poorly structured or support is insufficient.[^13][^20][^6][^8]

**Implementation challenges and outcomes.** Challenges include designing rigorous projects aligned with standards, assessing process and product, and ensuring all students participate meaningfully. Despite these, evidence places PBL/DBL in the strong evidence tier for improving academic achievement when well implemented.[^6][^8]


### Formative Assessment and Personalized Feedback

**Problem and theory.** Formative assessment involves frequent, low-stakes checks of understanding used to adapt instruction, based on Black and Wiliam’s work on assessment for learning. Personalized feedback tailors information about performance, strategies, and next steps to individual students, closely linked to SRL and mastery learning.[^42][^7]

**Evidence strength.** Meta-analytic work on dynamic assessment and feedback interventions (e.g., Swanson & Lussier’s synthesis; Visible Learning’s feedback and dynamic assessment influences) reports large effects on achievement (ES ≈ 1.12 for dynamic assessment; strong effects for feedback). Recent meta-analyses on feedback and SRL highlight that well-designed feedback can enhance self-regulation and performance, though categorization of feedback types and measurement standardization remain challenges.[^7][^42]

**Beneficiaries and conditions.** Personalized feedback is broadly beneficial, with higher effects when feedback is specific, process-focused, and accompanied by opportunities to act on it. For ADHD students, brief, immediate, behavior-contingent feedback and positive reinforcement are key components of effective classroom interventions. Feedback is most effective as part of ongoing formative assessment, not occasional comments.[^11][^20][^42][^7]

**Implementation challenges and outcomes.** Challenges include teacher workload, designing high-quality feedback, and ensuring students interpret and use feedback effectively. When integrated with mastery learning, RTI, and SRL, formative assessment and personalized feedback constitute core mechanisms of personalization with strong empirical support.[^42]


### Learning Analytics and AI-Assisted Personalized Learning

**Problem and theory.** Learning analytics uses data from digital platforms to model learner states and adapt content, while AI-assisted tutoring uses large language models or other AI systems to deliver personalized explanations, hints, and practice. These approaches aim to scale one-on-one tutoring benefits and provide real-time personalization.[^55][^18][^56]

**Evidence strength – pre-LLM systems.** Meta-analyses of intelligent tutoring systems (ITS) report average effect sizes around 0.40 compared to business-as-usual classroom instruction, indicating moderate gains. Large-scale implementations of AI-driven personalization in K–12 report improvements in standardized assessment scores, around 30% in some underserved contexts, though these are often quasi-experimental.[^18][^2]

**Evidence strength – LLM-based AI tutoring.** A randomized controlled trial of an LLM-based mathematics tutor (MathMentor-GPT) across 62 schools, involving 14,892 grade 7–9 students in four countries, found an overall effect of 0.27 SD on end-of-year math achievement, with larger effects for low prior achievers (0.41 SD) and students in under-resourced schools (0.38 SD). Another exploratory RCT integrating a supervised generative AI tutor (LearnLM) in UK secondary mathematics found interactive tutoring (human or AI-supervised) substantially outperformed static hints, with AI tutoring slightly surpassing human tutoring in immediate correction and knowledge transfer (ATE ≈ +5.5% probability of correct response on subsequent novel-topic questions).[^2][^57][^58][^3]

**Beneficiaries and conditions.** AI tutoring and learning analytics appear particularly beneficial for students with low prior achievement and in under-resourced schools, where additional personalized support is scarce. They are most effective when tightly integrated with teacher-led instruction, used as supplements rather than replacements, and constrained by pedagogical prompts and safety oversight.[^59][^58][^2][^3]

For ADHD, AI tutors can offer on-demand, patient explanations and immediate feedback, potentially supporting attention and motivation, but there is limited ADHD-specific randomized evidence and concerns about screen overuse and distraction.[^20]

**Implementation challenges and outcomes.** Challenges include equitable access to devices, data privacy, algorithmic bias, integration with curricula, and teacher training. When thoughtfully deployed, AI-assisted personalization can yield small-to-moderate achievement gains and improved remediation and misconception resolution, placing it currently in the moderate evidence tier, moving toward strong as more RCTs accumulate.[^18][^59]


## ADHD, Attention Difficulties, and Executive-Function Supports

### Achievement and Executive-Function Profiles in ADHD

Meta-analyses show that students with ADHD experience significant academic underachievement compared to peers, with sample-weighted effect sizes around d ≈ 0.71 for achievement gaps. Executive-function problems—especially in working memory, planning, and sustained attention—are common and linked to negative educational outcomes. These profiles justify personalization strategies targeting behavior, organization, and cognitive skills rather than relying solely on generalized classroom instruction.[^19][^60][^20]

### School-Based ADHD Interventions

**Behavioral and classroom interventions.** Meta-analyses of school-based psychosocial and behavioral interventions for ADHD report small-to-moderate effects on behavior and achievement. Visible Learning’s synthesis of intervention programs for ADHD (3 meta-analyses; 193 studies; 637 students; 2,333 effects) reports a weighted mean effect size around 0.28, suggesting likely positive impacts on achievement and behavior. Interventions include token economies, contingency management, peer tutoring, classroom accommodations, and parent training.[^11]

**Medication.** Meta-analyses of stimulant medication show small, positive impacts on academic achievement (weighted mean ES ≈ 0.30), with larger effects on behavioral outcomes and classroom functioning. One review found that methylphenidate’s direct impact on achievement tests is modest (ES ≈ 0.09), but it improves attention and on-task behavior, facilitating learning in structured environments.[^12]

**Executive-function and working-memory training.** A randomized controlled trial of an ecological executive skill training program for school-aged children with ADHD found improvements in executive functioning and some aspects of real-life functioning, including organization and planning. Another RCT of working-memory and executive-function interventions in school-aged children with ADHD reported gains in working memory and executive processes but mixed effects on symptom reduction and academic outcomes. Overall, EF training shows promise for improving task completion and organization but is not a standalone solution for core ADHD symptoms.[^9][^13]

### Instructional Adaptations with Strong Evidence for ADHD and Attention Difficulties

Combining findings from ADHD intervention meta-analyses, executive-function trials, RTI/MTSS research, and general instructional evidence yields several key adaptations:

- **Structured, explicit instruction:** Clear objectives, step-by-step modeling, guided practice, and frequent opportunities to respond improve attention and task completion for ADHD students and support academic achievement broadly.[^27][^7][^11]
- **Frequent, brief tasks and microlearning:** Short, varied tasks with immediate feedback help sustain attention and reduce procrastination, aligning with ADHD cognitive profiles.[^20][^11]
- **Behavioral supports and contingency management:** Token systems, behavior contracts, and reinforcement schedules improve on-task behavior and classroom engagement.[^12][^11]
- **Visual scaffolds and multimodal instruction:** Visual schedules, graphic organizers, and multimodal presentations support working memory and comprehension in ADHD and neurodiverse learners.[^31][^20]
- **Executive-function coaching and SRL interventions:** Goal-setting, self-monitoring, and organizational strategy instruction improve task completion and sometimes academic outcomes, with moderate effects when embedded in subject learning.[^9][^1][^13]
- **RTI/MTSS tiered supports:** Early, targeted reading and math interventions reduce risk of long-term academic failure for struggling students, including those with ADHD-related skill deficits.[^38][^36][^7]

These adaptations have stronger evidence when part of structured programs rather than isolated techniques.


## Case Studies of Personalized Learning at Scale

### Mastery Learning and Mastery-Based Systems in Districts

Historical and contemporary implementations of mastery learning in elementary and secondary classrooms have shown consistent gains in achievement and retention when schools adopt systematic formative assessment, corrective instruction, and flexible pacing. Districts implementing mastery-focused grading systems report reduced course failures and narrowed achievement gaps, though studies often use quasi-experimental designs and rely on test scores rather than long-term outcomes.[^5][^4][^21]

### Competency-Based Education Pilots

State-level CBE pilots (e.g., Colorado, New Hampshire, Illinois) show mixed but generally positive outcomes, including improved credit accumulation, reduced dropout rates, and perceptions of increased student agency, but rigorous causal studies are limited. Challenges include aligning state assessments with competency-based models and ensuring equity in flexible pacing.[^30][^15][^14]

### District-Wide MTSS Implementation (Wake County)

Wake County Public School System’s district-wide MTSS rollout was structured as a phased-in RCT across 88 schools, with treatment schools implementing MTSS starting in 2015–16 and control schools two years later. The initiative aimed to improve math and English scores and reduce behavioral suspensions; early findings highlight the importance of implementation fidelity, data systems, and professional development for realizing MTSS’s potential to improve achievement and behavior.[^39][^34]

### AI-Driven Personalized Learning in Underserved K–12 Systems

A systematic review of large-scale AI-driven personalized learning implementations in underserved K–12 contexts (multiple countries) reported roughly 30% improvement in standardized assessments for targeted populations, particularly low-performing and resource-constrained students. These implementations often combined adaptive platforms with teacher training and formative data use.[^18]

### Large-Scale LLM-Based AI Tutor Trial (MathMentor-GPT)

The MathMentor-GPT RCT across 62 schools in Nigeria, Spain, Ireland, and India (14,892 grade 7–9 students) demonstrated that an LLM-based math tutor integrated as weekly supplemental practice produced a 0.27 SD improvement in math achievement versus business-as-usual, with larger effects for low achievers and under-resourced schools. Teacher integration amplified effects, supporting the conclusion that AI tutors work best as complements to classroom teaching.[^2]

### Exploratory AI Tutor RCT in UK Secondary Mathematics (LearnLM)

An exploratory RCT in five UK secondary schools (165 students) using Eedi’s diagnostic platform found that supervised AI tutoring (LearnLM) performed comparably to expert human tutors and significantly better than static hints for immediate remediation, misconception resolution, and knowledge transfer. Supervising tutors approved over 76% of AI messages with minimal edits, and safety audits found no harmful content. The trial suggests that constrained, supervised AI tutors can scale high-quality, personalized support without compromising safety.[^57][^58][^3]


## Evidence Hierarchy for Personalized Learning Approaches

Based on meta-analytic effect sizes, randomized and quasi-experimental studies, and consistency of findings, the following hierarchy summarizes evidence strength:

### Strong Scientific Evidence

- **Mastery learning / mastery-based progression:** Multiple meta-analyses with moderate-to-large effects (ES ≈ 0.58–0.78; stronger in elementary and for low-ability students).[^4][^21][^5]
- **Response to Intervention (RTI) / MTSS (academic interventions):** Eight meta-analyses with weighted mean ES ≈ 0.73 and robust effects on reading and systemic outcomes.[^37][^36][^7]
- **Retrieval practice and spaced learning:** Large meta-analysis and classroom studies showing robust benefits for retention and transfer across age groups.[^49][^45][^46][^50][^51]
- **Self-regulated learning interventions in K–12:** Meta-analyses reporting ES ≈ 0.69 on academic performance, strategy use, and motivation.[^41][^40][^1]
- **Project-based and design-based learning (when well implemented):** Meta-analyses indicating medium-to-large positive effects (ES ≈ 0.50–0.71 for PBL; ≈ 0.60 for DBL).[^53][^8][^6]
- **Classroom interventions and psychosocial treatments for ADHD (combined behavioral/academic programs):** Meta-analyses showing small-to-moderate positive impacts on behavior and achievement (ES ≈ 0.26–0.31).[^12][^13][^11][^20]

### Moderate Scientific Evidence

- **Differentiated instruction in mixed-ability classrooms:** Systematic reviews and meta-syntheses showing generally positive impacts on engagement and achievement, especially for high-achieving students and in literacy, but with heterogeneous effect sizes and implementation variability.[^22][^23][^25][^16]
- **Explicit instruction / direct instruction:** Multiple meta-analyses (summarized in Visible Learning) indicating moderate-to-large effects, particularly in reading and math; strong conceptual and empirical support but often embedded within other models.[^21][^7][^27]
- **Universal Design for Learning (UDL) in K–12:** Systematic review of 13 studies showing potential for increased engagement and access, but mixed and variable outcomes.[^10][^31]
- **AI-assisted personalized learning and intelligent tutoring systems:** Meta-analyses and RCTs reporting moderate effects (ES ≈ 0.27–0.40) on achievement, with larger benefits for low-achievers and under-resourced schools.[^56][^3][^2][^18]
- **Executive-function and working-memory training for ADHD (school-based):** RCTs indicating improvements in EF and some academic or functional outcomes, but effects on core symptoms are modest and replication is limited.[^13][^9]

### Emerging or Mixed Evidence

- **Competency-based education (CBE) / systems-level competency progression:** Systematic review of 25 studies reporting mixed outcomes on achievement, motivation, and engagement, with serious challenges in isolating CBE effects and variable implementation.[^15][^14]
- **Universal Design for Learning integrated within MTSS:** Conceptually strong and supported by some quasi-experimental studies, but empirical evidence is still emerging.[^61][^33]
- **Broad “personalized learning” initiatives (non-specific):** District-level personalized learning reforms often show modest achievement gains, especially in math, but results are inconsistent, and fidelity varies.[^14][^18]
- **Gamification and student choice interventions:** Individual studies suggest positive effects on engagement and sometimes achievement, but meta-analytic evidence in K–12 is limited or mixed.[^8][^6]

### Popular Practices with Little or No Strong Scientific Support

- **Learning styles–based personalization:** Strong evidence indicates matching instruction to learning styles has negligible impact on achievement; this practice is widely used but empirically unsupported.[^27]
- **Unstructured “personalized learning” via self-paced software without strong pedagogical design:** Many implementations show limited or no gains, particularly when teacher involvement and formative assessment are minimal.[^15][^18][^14]
- **Generic “brain training” for attention and working memory in ADHD without curricular integration:** Evidence is mixed, with limited transfer to academic outcomes; such programs should be used cautiously.[^9][^20]


## Evidence-Based Framework for Personalized Learning in K–12

Drawing on the above evidence, an integrated framework for personalized learning—especially for students with ADHD and attention difficulties—should include the following components, with evidence levels noted.

### 1. Foundational High-Quality Instruction (Strong Evidence)

- **Explicit, structured instruction:** Use clear objectives, modeling, guided practice, and frequent checks for understanding. This supports all students and is particularly beneficial for ADHD and executive-function challenges.[^7][^27]
- **Mastery-based progression:** Implement frequent formative assessments and corrective instruction; require mastery before advancing, especially in foundational literacy and numeracy.[^5][^4][^21]
- **Retrieval practice and spaced review:** Incorporate frequent low-stakes quizzes, cumulative reviews, and spaced practice, emphasizing delayed retrieval to consolidate long-term memory.[^45][^46][^50][^51][^49]

### 2. Tiered Supports via RTI/MTSS (Strong Evidence)

- **Tier 1:** Ensure evidence-based core instruction in reading and math using explicit, mastery-oriented teaching and frequent formative assessment.[^35][^7]
- **Tier 2:** Provide small-group, targeted interventions for students at risk (including ADHD students with skill deficits), using structured programs with demonstrated efficacy (e.g., systematic phonics, math problem-solving interventions).[^62][^36][^38]
- **Tier 3:** Offer individualized, intensive supports for nonresponders, possibly including one-on-one tutoring, more frequent sessions, and comprehensive behavioral/academic plans.[^37][^7]

### 3. Self-Regulation, Metacognition, and Executive-Function Supports (Strong to Moderate Evidence)

- **SRL strategy instruction:** Explicitly teach goal-setting, planning, monitoring, and reflection within subject areas, modeling strategies and providing guided practice.[^44][^40][^1]
- **Executive-function coaching for ADHD:** Embed organizational skills, time management, and task initiation supports into classroom routines, with visual schedules and checklists.[^13][^20][^9]
- **Personalized feedback:** Provide specific, process-oriented feedback linked to SRL and mastery goals, using formative assessment data.[^42][^7]

### 4. High-Engagement Pedagogies with Strong Evidence

- **Project-based and design-based learning:** Use well-scaffolded projects and design tasks aligned with standards, ensuring sufficient instructional time and explicit teaching of necessary content and strategies.[^53][^6][^8]
- **Cognitive apprenticeship elements:** Model expert thinking, provide coaching and scaffolding, and gradually fade support, particularly in STEM and complex problem-solving domains.[^6][^8]

For ADHD and attention difficulties, projects should be broken into manageable sub-tasks with clear deadlines, rubrics, and check-ins to prevent overload.[^20][^13]

### 5. Technology-Enhanced Personalization and AI (Moderate Evidence)

- **Adaptive practice platforms:** Employ systems that adjust difficulty, provide immediate feedback, and supply mastery data, especially in mathematics and language arts.[^56][^2][^18]
- **AI-assisted tutoring:** Integrate constrained, supervised AI tutors that provide hints, explanations, and Socratic questioning for students during independent work, prioritizing low achievers and under-resourced contexts.[^58][^3][^2]
- **Learning analytics:** Use platform data to identify struggling students, monitor progress, and inform RTI tier decisions, while maintaining human oversight.[^55][^18]

These tools should complement, not replace, teacher judgment and explicit instruction; evidence supports moderate gains in achievement and remediation when integrated thoughtfully.

### 6. ADHD-Specific Instructional Adaptations (Strong to Moderate Evidence)

For students with ADHD and attention difficulties, the framework should incorporate:

- **Short, varied tasks (microlearning):** Use brief exercises with clear start/finish, interleaved practice, and immediate feedback to sustain attention and reduce task avoidance.[^11][^20]
- **Multimodal instruction and visual scaffolds:** Present information using visual, auditory, and kinesthetic modes; use graphic organizers, timelines, and visual schedules to support working memory.[^10][^31][^20]
- **Behavioral supports:** Implement token systems, reinforcement, and consistent routines to improve engagement and task completion.[^12][^11]
- **Structured routines and transitions:** Maintain predictable classroom routines, clear signals for transitions, and advanced organizers to reduce executive-function load.[^13][^20]
- **Collaboration with medical and psychological supports:** Coordinate with healthcare providers regarding medication and comorbid conditions, recognizing that medication can modestly improve academic performance mainly by enhancing attention.[^12][^20]

### 7. System-Level Design and Professional Learning (Emerging to Strong Evidence)

- **UDL-informed curriculum design:** Apply UDL principles to ensure curricula offer multiple means of engagement, representation, and expression, thereby embedding personalization for diverse learners, including ADHD and neurodiverse students.[^33][^31][^10]
- **Competency-based progression where feasible:** Use clear competency statements and flexible pacing in subjects where mastery is critical (e.g., math), ensuring high-quality assessments and equity safeguards.[^14][^15]
- **Ongoing teacher professional development:** Provide training in SRL, formative assessment, DI, RTI/MTSS, and AI tools, as teacher competence consistently moderates effectiveness of personalized approaches.[^34][^16][^1]


## Conclusion

The scientific literature in educational psychology and learning sciences supports a nuanced view of personalized learning: personalization is not a single program but a constellation of practices—mastery learning, retrieval practice, RTI/MTSS, SRL, project-based learning, explicit instruction, and targeted ADHD supports—that, when coherently combined, can substantially improve learning outcomes, particularly for students who struggle in traditional classrooms. High-quality evidence shows that structured, mastery-oriented, feedback-rich, and cognitively informed personalization consistently improves achievement, retention, engagement, and motivation; emerging RCTs suggest that AI-assisted tutoring and learning analytics can extend these benefits at scale when used responsibly.[^3][^1][^2][^4][^18][^53][^7][^11]

At the same time, popular labels such as "personalized learning" or "learning styles" can be misleading if they are not anchored in proven mechanisms; practices lacking strong empirical support should be treated cautiously and subordinated to evidence-based components. For K–12 systems designing personalized learning initiatives—especially those concerned with ADHD and attention difficulties—the priority should be to implement and integrate the high-evidence strategies outlined above, monitor outcomes systematically, and iteratively refine practice based on data and research rather than relying solely on branding or technology.[^27][^14]
